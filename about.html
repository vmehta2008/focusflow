FocusFlow

A study app that
tells you the truth

Most study apps are timers with a streak counter. This one knows what you're studying, what you've forgotten, how you slept, and what you're avoiding — and it says so plainly, even when that isn't what you want to hear.

The problem

Nobody fails from lack of apps

A student sits down with four hours and loses forty minutes deciding what to open. They study the chapter they like. They mark things done that they half-know. Three weeks later the first chapter has quietly evaporated, and nothing told them.

Every one of those failures is a memory problem, not a motivation problem. Software is good at memory.
What it does

🎯 Decides for you

One button. It weighs what's overdue for revision, what you scored badly on, and what's closest to an exam — then names one chapter.

Deciding burns the willpower you needed for the studying.

🔁 Remembers what you forgot

Spaced repetition, running quietly. The gap widens as a chapter sticks and snaps shut when it doesn't.

You never think about when to revise. It just appears.

🕒 Plans around your actual life

Gym at seven, home by eight, and half an hour before you can think straight. It schedules around all of it — including the settling-down.

A plan you can keep beats an impressive one you can't.

✈️ Follows you to Telegram

Asks how you got on each morning. Answers doubts at midnight where you already are. Messages you if you disappear for three days.

The rules it was built on
One

Silence is the worst thing software can do

A bot that says nothing is indistinguishable from a broken one — because it is a broken one. Every message gets a reply, every failure names itself, and "I don't know" is a complete answer.

So: tap anything, and something happens. Even when everything behind it is on fire.
Two

A confident wrong answer is worse than no answer

Ask about a section number or a deadline and two different models answer independently. Where they agree, you get one clean answer. Where they disagree, you're shown both, marked — and told that's the line to trust least.

Anything about a syllabus or an exam date is looked up live, never recited from memory.

So: you're never quietly handed a guess dressed as a fact.
Three

Flattery is not encouragement

The weekly review counts what actually happened. If you did no revision at all, it says: that's the pattern that feels productive in September and hurts in May.

There's a button for admitting you marked something done that you hadn't. It resets the chapter and says nothing else.

So: the numbers stay worth looking at.
Four

A student is a person, not an output

Sleep, meals, mood and weight sit beside the chapters, because a plan built on five hours of sleep isn't a plan. On Sundays it asks how you've been rather than what you've finished.

The weight tracker will never set a calorie target, show a BMI, or tell you a number is good or bad. If it sees weight dropping fast during exam season it says something kind and points you at a real person.

So: nothing here can quietly make you unwell.
Five

Use the better tool, don't imitate it

NotebookLM writes better quizzes than this app does — it's free and it reads PDFs. So instead of competing, one button packages your notes for it, and another logs the score you got.

Your teacher explains a chapter better than any model. So it asks who you follow, then looks at their channel first.

So: it makes the things you already use work together.
Six

Your data is yours, with all that implies

Nothing is uploaded. There's no account to make, no password to forget, nobody holding your marks. Every AI key is your own, stored on your device only.

Which cuts both ways, honestly: a browser clean-up would take everything. That's why there's a backup button, and why the app nags you about it.

So: nobody can sell it, leak it, or lock you out of it — including us.
Who it's for

Anyone with a syllabus and not enough time

It ships knowing CA, JEE, NEET, UPSC, school boards and more. But if your goal isn't in the list, you type it in plain words — "I want to be an army officer" — and it goes and finds which exam that is, who conducts it, what the stages are, and what's actually on the paper.

🗣 It speaks your language

English, हिन्दी, Hinglish, or both at once. Voice notes work too — half of what you'd say at eleven at night you'd never type.

👨‍👩‍👧 More than one of you

Separate profiles on one device, each with their own data. Siblings don't share a backlog.

📋 Something to show for it

One page — hours, streak, attendance, what's behind — that you can hand a parent or a teacher, or send on Telegram. It prints the real numbers, including the bad ones, and carries nothing private with it.

How it writes

Every answer, held to the same three people

A study app that answers questions is only as good as the worst answer it gives at midnight. So there is one standing brief, attached to every answer the app produces — a doubt, a chapter's notes, a mindmap, a practice test — rather than one standard per button.

🎓 The topper who wrote it

The answer first, not a wind-up. The section, standard or formula named. One worked line with real numbers. And at the end, the mistakes people actually lose marks on.

🔍 The checker

No section number, date, rate or case unless it's sure of it. Where a thing changes by syllabus year or board, it says which. Where it can't verify, it tells you to check the bare Act rather than inventing a confident number.

👩‍🏫 The teacher who marks it

Re-read as an examiner before you see it: is the order the order a marker expects, is a step missing, would a weaker student go wrong on any line.

None of that makes a model infallible. It's why every answer is also put past a second opinion when you've given it two keys, and why disagreements are shown rather than smoothed over.

The honest part

What it won't do

It won't study for you

It removes the deciding, the forgetting and the guessing. The sitting down is still entirely yours.

It won't always be right

That's why two models check each other and why it shows you where they disagreed instead of picking one.

It won't hold your data for you

Nothing is on a server — that's why nobody can read it, and why nobody can get it back either. On an iPhone, Safari deletes a website's stored data after about a week of not opening it, so the app says so and shows you how to stop it: put it on your Home Screen, and save a backup file. Both take one tap.
